Shrimp Cultivation in Bangladesh: Adaptation or Maladaptation?

A slideshow explainer of the review paper by Nahian Ibnat. Use the arrow keys or the buttons below to move through it.

The question

Shrimp earns money. Is it a smart response to a changing coast?

Salt water is moving inland in southwest Bangladesh, and shrimp farming took off to profit from it. The paper asks two things:

Adaptation or maladaptation?Does shrimp cultivation help coastal people cope, or make things worse?
Can it be sustainable?Is it possible to grow shrimp for export without the damage?

Method: peer-reviewed primary research since 2000, found through Google Scholar, SAGE and Cambridge Core.

The boom

A huge export market drove rapid growth

World Bank, ADBbacked the growth with policy changes and incentives

The cost

Damage on the land and in the water

Salt in the soilLong flooding with salt water can leave land infertile within a year or two.
Fewer crops and fishLocal rice gave way to salt-tolerant hybrids. Spinach, mango, guava and koi fish are disappearing in Satkhira.
Polluted waterFeed, antibiotics and chemicals are used without proper dosing knowledge and drain into the environment.
Thirsty farmingAbout 50 to 60 million litres of water per tonne of shrimp, half of it fresh. Drinking water becomes scarce.

The cost

Mangroves are lost, and their carbon with them

The Sundarbans is the world's largest compact mangrove forest. Clearing mangroves for shrimp ponds, as in Chakaria, releases the blue carbon stored in their soil and trees.

160 to 360 t/hablue carbon stored in the Sundarbans, depending on plant type and salinity
Less protectionWithout mangroves, shrimp farms are exposed to storm surges too.

The cost

"Good for the rich and bad for the poor"

Shrimp expansion deepened class and gender inequality. Land leased or sold under pressure moved to powerful farmers, and common land used for fishing and grazing was taken over. Land disputes in Satkhira and Khulna turned violent.

The landless and small farmers lost sharecropping and were pushed into poorly paid labour.

The verdict

Maladaptation

Shrimp farming pays today, but the damage to land, water, mangroves and equality is largely irreversible. The cost-benefit analysis shows a success now that is unlikely to last.

The authors note the real problem is unplanned, unregulated farming rather than shrimp itself, and policy can address that.

The way forward

A path to sustainable shrimp

ICZM (main proposal)Integrated Coastal Zone Management: a dedicated shrimp law, one lead agency, and community monitoring.
Rain harvestingClean drinking water and salt-free irrigation.
Mangrove restorationRestores blue carbon storage and storm protection.
Salt-tolerant cropsSaline and drought-tolerant seeds and short-duration rice.
New speciesPacific white shrimp worked on saline land in India and could be tried here.
ZoningMatch shrimp farming to low, medium or high salinity areas.

Recommendations

What policymakers should do

  • Register all shrimp farms so they can be monitored.
  • Choose farm inputs that do not harm the environment, and improve health and post-harvest practices.
  • Diversify into other brackish-water species such as mangrove crab.
  • Implement ICZM and launch mangrove restoration projects.
  • Support saline-tolerant and drought-tolerant crop seeds.

Bottom line

Keep the gains, limit the harm

Shrimp farming grew fast on export demand without enough planning or regulation. With no specific shrimp policy, local people do not back the industry. ICZM offers the clearest way to protect the economic benefits while reducing the social and environmental costs.
