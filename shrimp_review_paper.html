Adaptation or Maladaptation?

Abstract

Summary Shrimp farming has grown into Bangladesh's second-largest export industry, generating over $445 million annually and employing more than two million people. Yet this growth has come without adequate planning or regulation. This paper reviews existing literature to assess whether shrimp cultivation functions as a genuine climate adaptation strategy or as a form of maladaptation — and identifies pathways toward sustainable aquaculture through Integrated Coastal Zone Management (ICZM), rain harvesting, mangrove restoration, and new crop and species varieties.

Background & Scale

Since the mid-1980s, shrimp has been one of Bangladesh's most important export commodities. Favorable agro-climatic conditions in the coastal southwest, combined with pressure from international institutions like the World Bank and the Asian Development Bank, drove a dramatic expansion of shrimp aquaculture across the country.

This expansion was driven by structural adjustment policies tied to trade liberalization, and the high profit potential of shrimp farming attracted investors ranging from individual smallholders to multinational corporations.

The Economic Case for Shrimp Farming

The economic rationale for expanding shrimp farming was not unfounded. Shrimp aquaculture created a wide range of livelihood options for coastal communities — in growing, processing, selling, and exporting. For small farmers initially facing salt intrusion on their rice paddy fields, switching to shrimp ponds offered a practical short-term income strategy.

Key Point Shrimp is Bangladesh's second-largest export earner after garments, and it was promoted as a pro-poor growth mechanism by national and international policy bodies.

At the macro level, shrimp farming has generated significant foreign exchange and contributed to GDP growth. The government and wealthier landowners captured the largest share of this benefit — a pattern this paper critically examines.

Why It Is Maladaptation

Despite short-term economic gains, unregulated shrimp farming has produced severe, often irreversible environmental and social consequences that meet the definition of maladaptation — an action that reduces long-term adaptive capacity.

Salt Intrusion & Soil Degradation

Shrimp ponds require saline water as an input. When land is flooded by saltwater for extended periods, salinity percolates into surrounding soils, destroying their fertility. This prevents free nitrogen fixation, leaving land infertile within one to two years. In Parulia, Satkhira, local varieties of fruits and vegetables have been pushed toward extinction.

Water Quality Deterioration

Shrimp farms rely heavily on artificial feeds, antibiotics, and disinfectants. These chemicals enter surrounding water systems through pond exchanges, creating toxicity risks for non-target species and human populations. Disease outbreaks driven by antibiotic resistance have caused significant shrimp mortality and yield losses.

Mangrove Forest Loss

Blue Carbon at Risk The Sundarbans — the world's largest mangrove forest — stores 160–360 tonnes of blue carbon per hectare, valued at over US$1.87 billion globally. Shrimp farming has destroyed over 1.2 million acres of mangrove, releasing stored carbon and removing a critical natural barrier against storm surges.

Mangroves in Bangladesh fell from 452,444 ha in 1976 to 441,455 ha in 2010. The Chakaria Sundarbans in southeast Bangladesh, which held 8,500 acres of mangroves, were entirely cleared for shrimp ponds.

Socio-economic Inequality

Shrimp farming has widened existing class and gender inequalities. Large landowners expanded their holdings by forcing small farmers — through land disputes and sometimes violence — to sell or lease their plots. Government-owned land previously used for fishing, grazing, and subsistence farming has been converted by powerful shrimp operators, leaving the rural poor more vulnerable than before.

Who Wins, Who Loses

The distributional consequences of shrimp farming are uneven across stakeholder groups, and critically, even the current "winners" face long-term losses as the industry becomes unsustainable.

In economic terms, shrimp farming follows a diminishing marginal product trajectory. Beyond a threshold, each additional hectare converted to shrimp ponds yields a smaller net benefit — and the negative externalities accumulate faster than the gross returns, representing a classic case of market failure requiring policy intervention.

Paths Toward Sustainable Shrimp Farming

The literature identifies several practical interventions. The most comprehensive framework is Integrated Coastal Zone Management (ICZM), which provides the institutional architecture to coordinate the others.

Integrated Coastal Zone Management (ICZM)

ICZM addresses three dimensions: policy integration (a specific legal framework for shrimp), functional integration (a single dedicated agency with oversight responsibility), and system integration (genuine participation from local farming communities in policy design and monitoring). Currently no such framework exists — shrimp farming sits awkwardly between fishery and agricultural regulations.

Rain Harvesting

Provides clean freshwater to coastal communities affected by salinization, reducing dependency on contaminated groundwater.

Mangrove Restoration

Government-led projects can rebuild the Sundarbans' carbon sink function and restore the coastal barrier that shrimp farms depend on.

Saline-Tolerant Crops

High-yield variety (HYV) rice and drought-tolerant seeds allow farmers on degraded land to continue productive agriculture.

Penaeus Vannamei (White Shrimp)

This Pacific species grows faster, tolerates higher stocking density, and withstands a wider salinity range — a more sustainable alternative.

Recommendations

For Policymakers

  • Register all shrimp farms under a national monitoring system to enable safe, traceable production.
  • Mandate environmental screening of all farm inputs — feeds, antibiotics, disinfectants — before use.
  • Implement ICZM as the overarching governance framework for coastal aquaculture.
  • Launch government-funded mangrove restoration programs across affected coastal districts.
  • Subsidize saline-tolerant and drought-tolerant crop seed distribution for displaced smallholder farmers.
  • Promote brackish-water aquaculture diversification — including mangrove crab — to reduce shrimp monoculture risk.
  • Pilot commercial cultivation of Penaeus Vannamei in inland saline zones, following the Indian model.

For Future Researchers

  • Longitudinal studies on soil salinity recovery timelines following shrimp pond decommissioning.
  • Quantitative cost-benefit analysis that prices in environmental externalities (carbon release, biodiversity loss, water contamination).
  • Gender-disaggregated analysis of livelihood impact for women in shrimp-farming communities.
  • Assessment of Penaeus Vannamei viability under Bangladesh's specific coastal hydrology.

Conclusion

Shrimp farming in Bangladesh began as a pragmatic response to salt intrusion and a route to foreign exchange earnings. In the short run, it delivered on both. But the rapid, unplanned, and unregulated expansion that followed has produced negative externalities — soil degradation, water contamination, mangrove destruction, and growing inequality — that now outweigh the benefits for most of the population affected.

The industry's current trajectory is not viable. Countries that develop sustainable aquaculture practices will retain international market access; Bangladesh risks losing its competitive position if it does not reform. ICZM, combined with community participation, mangrove restoration, and investment in alternative species and crops, offers a realistic route to making shrimp farming work for the long term.

Core Argument Shrimp cultivation in Bangladesh is currently a maladaptation strategy. It transfers climate risk from rich farmers and the government to the rural poor and the coastal environment. Without structural policy reform, the sector's short-term gains will be erased by long-term environmental and economic costs.
